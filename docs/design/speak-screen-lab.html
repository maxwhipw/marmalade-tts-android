now The screen as it ships today

Reproduced from SpeakScreen.kt so the comparison is fair: outlined voice chip, then a FlowRow of alias FilterChips plus a trailing “Create alias” AssistChip, then Speak, then a permanent “Ready”. Two competing chip languages stacked on top of each other, no hierarchy between them, and in dark mode the pale chips are the loudest thing on the page — which is exactly the awkwardness.

  • Two chip shapes, one job. The outlined chip is the current voice; the filled chips are aliases that also set the voice. Same visual weight class, different meanings, no label explaining either.
  • No hierarchy. Nothing says which alias is primary, and the alias you picked looks identical to the one you never use.
  • Everything hidden. Engine, speed, effect and cloud-vs-device are all invisible here — they only exist on the Aliases screen.
  • “Ready” is noise. It's on screen 99% of the time and says nothing.

A Persona card

One elevated card that is the current persona: avatar, name, primary badge, engine › voice with the cloud glyph, and a meta row for speed / effect / language. Tapping anywhere on the card (or “Change”) opens the persona sheet. The chip row disappears entirely.

For

This is the only direction that answers “what will I actually hear?” before you press Speak. Engine, voice, speed, effect and network-dependency are all on the surface, so the card doubles as a pre-flight check — and “Gradium › Adam ☁ · 1.00× · No effect” is genuinely useful when you have six near-identical personas. It's a single object with a single tap target, which reads far calmer than a wrapped chip field, and it grows to any number of aliases without reflowing the layout. It also matches the alias cards on the Aliases screen, so the two screens finally speak the same language.

Against

It's the biggest block of the five and it pushes Speak down the page — on a short phone the button starts flirting with the fold, which is a real cost on the one screen whose whole job is “type, tap”. Switching persona now costs two taps and a sheet animation where today it costs one, and Max's stated love of the screen is that it's minimal; a card with five data points is the least minimal option here. The meta chips also risk becoming decoration you stop reading after a week, at which point you've paid the height for nothing.

B Current-voice row → persona sheet recommended

A single 56 dp row directly under the input: avatar, persona name, engine · voice in secondary text, cloud glyph if it's a network voice, chevron. Tap opens a bottom sheet listing every persona with the same three facts, plus “New persona”. Nothing else on the screen.

For

It's the smallest possible control that still names what it does — one line, one tap target, reads left-to-right like a sentence, and its meaning is unambiguous in a way the current two-chip-languages arrangement never is. It scales to fifty personas without touching the home screen's height or layout, which the chip row emphatically does not. The sheet is where richness belongs: full names, engine, cloud glyph, search later if the list grows, and “New persona” living at the bottom instead of squatting in the main screen as a permanent chip. It preserves the minimalism Max says he likes while removing the exact element he says looks awkward, and it's the least code: one row composable plus a ModalBottomSheet reusing the Aliases list rows.

Against

Switching persona goes from one tap to two, and if you genuinely ping-pong between two voices all day that's a real tax — the chip row's one virtue is that your top three aliases are always one tap away. It also hides the roster: a new user can no longer see at a glance that personas exist at all, so discovery leans entirely on the row's chevron affordance. And a sheet is one more surface to theme, animate and test.

C Segmented switcher

One fixed-height pill track split into segments — your first three personas plus an overflow “···” that opens the full list. Selected segment is Toast; cloud-backed personas carry the glyph inline. A single caption under the track spells out what's selected.

For

It keeps the one-tap switch that the chip row's only real advantage, but replaces a ragged wrapped field with one rigid object of fixed height — so the layout never reflows, Speak never moves, and there's exactly one control instead of “outlined chip plus N filled chips plus a create chip”. Segmented controls also carry the right semantics: these options are mutually exclusive, which is precisely true of personas and which the current FilterChip row fails to communicate. The caption underneath restores the engine and cloud information the chips throw away, for one line of height.

Against

It doesn't survive long names: “Maximilian” and “Documentary Narrator” in 412 dp across three segments means truncation on day one, and truncated persona names are worse than no names. Three visible slots is an arbitrary cut, so the roster becomes “first three by creation date plus a mystery ···”, and the overflow is a second-class citizen that will hold most of your personas the moment you have five. Material 3's segmented button also isn't really meant for user-generated variable-width content, so it'll fight you.

D Primary pinned + avatar micro-rail

Leans on something the app already knows: SettingsRepository.primaryAliasName. The primary persona gets a real row with name, engine, speed and cloud glyph; every other persona collapses to a 32 dp initial in a micro-rail underneath, with a dashed “+”. Tap an initial to switch (it swaps into the pinned row), long-press to make it primary.

For

It's the only direction that encodes the hierarchy that already exists in the data — one persona is primary, the rest are alternates — instead of pretending all N are equal the way the chip row does. That means one-tap switching survives, but the layout cost of the alternates drops to a single 32 dp line no matter how many you have, so it's both denser and more informative than today. Circular initials read as “people/personas” instantly, and hanging the cloud glyph off the avatar corner as a badge gives you per-persona network legibility for free, at a glance, across the whole roster.

Against

Initials are ambiguous — two personas starting with “M” look identical, and unlike a contacts app there's no photo to disambiguate them, so you're relying on position memory. That's fine at three personas and bad at eight. It also introduces two interaction grammars in the same block (tap to select, long-press to re-pin) with no visible affordance for the second, and “which one is pinned vs which one is active” becomes a state you have to draw carefully or it reads as two selections at once.

E Persona carousel

A horizontally swipeable dial of avatars centred on the active persona — the centre one is 56 dp with its full name below, neighbours shrink and fade. Page dots underneath. Swipe to change, tap a neighbour to jump, “+” lives at the end of the reel.

For

By far the most characterful option and the best fit for the mascot-led, warm personality of the brand — personas genuinely are characters, and a dial of faces sells that in a way a chip never will. Swiping is a zero-tap gesture with a large target, and the focus/blur treatment gives an unmistakable answer to “which one is active” with no selection chrome at all. It's also the most delightful thing on the roadmap for a screen that's currently four rectangles, and it stays exactly one row tall regardless of roster size.

Against

It's the wrong control for a settings-shaped decision. Carousels hide their contents, make “go to the fifth persona” a multi-swipe chore, and are notoriously poor for accessibility and TalkBack ordering. It puts a horizontal-swipe target directly under a multi-line text field, which invites gesture conflicts, and the fade-to-40% neighbours are borderline on contrast in both modes. It's the option most likely to be loved in a mockup and resented in daily use, and it solves “awkward” by adding flourish rather than by adding clarity.

status The status line: silent when idle

Per the brief — idle says nothing. The 20 dp slot stays reserved so the bottom nav doesn't jump when a message arrives, but it renders empty. The line only speaks when it has something real: speaking, loading a model, an error, or a missing engine (which stays an actionable TextButton in error colour, as it is today). Change in statusText(): PlaybackState.Idle → "".

Rules

  • Idle → empty string. Not “Ready”, not a dash. Absence is the success state.
  • Reserve the height, don't reserve the text. A fixed 20 dp minHeight keeps Speak and the nav bar still when a message appears or clears.
  • Only two colours. onSurfaceVariant for transient progress, error for anything the user must act on. Nothing gets a success colour.
  • Model-missing stays a button, not a sentence — it's the one status that has a fix one tap away.

cloud The cloud glyph

Roughly half the voice roster is network-backed (Venice / OpenAI) and half runs on-device. That difference decides whether a persona can fail on a train, whether it costs money, and whether it leaves the phone — so it deserves a persistent mark, not a word buried on the Aliases screen. The glyph is a 14 dp filled cloud in onSurfaceVariant: quiet enough to ignore, present enough to scan. On-device gets nothing — absence is the default, which keeps the common case clean and makes the marked case pop.

pick What I'd ship B, with one borrowed idea from D

Ship B — the current-voice row plus a persona sheet. It removes the awkward element outright rather than restyling it, it keeps the screen as minimal as Max says he wants it, and it's the only direction whose height is constant no matter how many personas exist. Everything rich moves into the sheet, where there's room for it and where it doesn't tax the ninety-nine-times-out-of-a-hundred case of “type, tap Speak”.
Borrow the primary badge from D. The app already stores a primary alias and the home screen currently ignores it. Marking it in the sheet (and defaulting the row to it) costs nothing and recovers the hierarchy that the flat chip row destroys.
Not C, not E. C truncates real persona names inside a week and turns the roster into “first three plus a mystery button”. E is the prettiest mock here and would be the first thing to annoy you at persona number five — plus a horizontal swipe target sitting directly under a multi-line text field is asking for gesture conflicts.
A is the honourable runner-up and the right answer if the pre-flight information turns out to matter more than height — it's also the smallest jump from B, since B's row is just A's card with the meta line deleted. Build B first; if you miss the detail, A is a one-file expansion of the same component.

Ship list, in order

  • 1 · Status line goes silent. One line in statusText(), zero risk, immediate win. Keep the reserved height.
  • 2 · Cloud glyph everywhere. Add it to the existing “Cloud” MetaChip on the Aliases screen and to whatever the Speak screen ends up showing. One shared composable, so the two screens can't drift.
  • 3 · Replace both chip rows with the B row. Delete AliasChipRow and the standalone AssistChip; one row replaces both.
  • 4 · Persona sheet. ModalBottomSheet reusing the Aliases list rows, primary badged, “New persona” at the bottom routing to the existing alias editor.
  • 5 · Leave the wordmark, mascot, input and Speak button alone. They are not the problem and every mock above keeps them pixel-identical on purpose.
